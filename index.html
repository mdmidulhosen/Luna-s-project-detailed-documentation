Real Estate Marketplace — Technical Proposal & Architecture

September 23, 2026 · Prepared by Kibria and team · Draft for discussion

1. Executive summary

We can build this real-estate marketplace on the stack you chose: React, Node.js, PostgreSQL + PostGIS, Leaflet + OpenStreetMap, hosted on Infomaniak in Switzerland. Most of your capacity targets are achievable. Several of them depend on infrastructure, operations and testing, not on the code alone, and we say so clearly for each one.

The main conclusions:

  • The software is free of licence fees; running it is not free. Every core component is open source. Servers, photo storage, bandwidth, backups, email delivery and the domain are real monthly costs.
  • OpenStreetMap's data is free, but its public map servers are not for a site of this size. At your traffic level we must host the map tiles ourselves, on your Infomaniak servers. This keeps you independent of Google Maps, Mapbox and similar providers.
  • Photos are the largest part of the system. 4 million photos means roughly 2–8 TB of storage before copies and backups. Photos go into object storage, never into the database.
  • 99.9% uptime and 2-hour recovery need a redundant setup and a tested recovery plan. Daily backups alone do not deliver either target.
  • 9 million visits a month is a realistic design target, not an automatic guarantee. We design for it from day one and prove it with load tests before launch.

On price and timeline. Our earlier figure ($10,500, 90–100 days) was given before you shared the capacity targets. It fits a small listings website, not the system described here. Section 19 gives a revised, itemised estimate split into two phases.

2. Our understanding of the project

You want a real-estate-only classifieds marketplace: simple, clean and fast, in the spirit of Gumtree, but an original product. Other sectors are out of scope for now. The platform must be built on open-source software, hosted independently in Switzerland, and designed from the start for national-portal traffic.

What the platform is

  • A place where private owners and real-estate companies publish properties for rent or sale.
  • A place where visitors search by location, price and property type, in a list or on a map.
  • A system that can load large inventories from spreadsheets (CSV/Excel).
  • A system where listings stay live for 30 days and then expire automatically.

What the platform is not (in this phase)

  • Not a multi-sector marketplace (cars, jobs, goods). The design allows adding sectors later.
  • Not connected to other property portals or agency software through live APIs.
  • Not a native mobile app. The website will be fully responsive on phones. Native apps are a future option (section 20).

Question 2 — 30-day cycle with timestamps and a cron job

Answer: YES (FACT). This is standard, reliable engineering.

Each listing stores published_at and expires_at (published date + 30 days). A scheduled job runs every hour, finds listings past expires_at, and marks them expired. Expired listings disappear from search and the map immediately.

We recommend not deleting records instantly. An expired listing is first hidden and archived, then permanently deleted after a retention period you choose (for example 90 days). This protects against accidental loss, supports disputes and renewals, and keeps the database safe. Full design in section 7.

Question 4 — CSV/Excel files instead of APIs

Answer: YES, WITH CONDITIONS. You do not need API connections to other platforms to launch, if your property inventory arrives as CSV or Excel files and you do not need live synchronisation with other portals or agency software.

One correction: "a simple script will do the job perfectly" is not realistic. Spreadsheets from different companies contain missing addresses, wrong prices, duplicate rows and different column names. The job needs a proper import pipeline with validation, a preview, error reports and address geocoding (section 10). APIs become necessary only later, if agencies want their listings to update automatically.

Question 5 — Does a lightweight database guarantee speed and crash resistance?

Answer: NO, not by itself. A clean, text-and-pins data model helps a lot. It does not guarantee speed or stability at your scale.

What actually makes the site fast and stable:

  • Correct indexes, including a spatial index for map queries.
  • Photos stored outside the database, in object storage.
  • Caching of popular searches and pages.
  • Pagination, so no page ever loads thousands of listings.
  • Several application servers behind a load balancer.
  • Monitoring and alerts.
  • Load tests that prove the targets before launch.

The biggest load on your platform will not be the listing text. It will be photos and map requests.

Question 7 — 9 million visits a month, automatically, without crashing or a rewrite?

Answer: NO as stated. YES if we design and test for it.

The stack itself (Node.js, PostgreSQL, PostGIS) runs sites far larger than this. The risk is not the technology. It is how the system is built and operated.

What we commit to: an architecture that can grow by adding servers, not rewriting code. The application servers keep no user state, so we can run two, four or ten of them. Photos and maps are served separately from the application. The database can add read copies.

What we cannot honestly promise:

  • "Automatically": Infomaniak offers autoscaling, but it must be configured, tested and paid for. Adding capacity is a planned action, usually minutes, not automatic magic.
  • "Without crashing": every system can fail. We design to fail gracefully and recover fast (sections 13–14).
  • "Never a rewrite": no one can promise that for every future feature. We can make a rewrite very unlikely for the traffic levels you have described.

The proof is a load test before launch against your targets: 300 requests per second and your peak map scenario (section 16).

4. Capacity analysis

Your targets are realistic for this stack, with one exception: the 15,000 map users and the 300 requests per second describe different loads, and we need to agree which one we design for. All figures below are engineering estimates, to be confirmed by load testing.

Summary

Target What it means in practice Assessment
9M visits/month About 300,000/day, about 3.5 visits per second on average. Peak hours typically run 2–3× the average. YES, WITH CONDITIONS
300 requests/second Consistent with 9M visits if it counts application requests (search, listing pages, login), not photos or map tiles. YES, WITH CONDITIONS — proven by load test
15,000 map users at once Depends on the definition (below). NEEDS YOUR DECISION
200,000 listings Roughly 1–2 GB of database data including indexes. Small for PostgreSQL. FACT (estimate)
4,000,000 photos 2–12 TB depending on photo size and whether originals are kept. YES, WITH CONDITIONS — object storage

From visits to requests

A visit is one person's session. A typical visit on a listings site views about 4–6 pages, and each page makes several application requests. At 9M visits a month, that averages roughly 50–100 application requests per second, with peaks of about 150–300. Your 300 requests-per-second target is a sensible peak design figure for application traffic.

Photos and map tiles are separate and much more numerous. A single visit can load 30 or more images. These are served by a caching layer and object storage, not by the application servers, so they do not count toward the 300.

What "15,000 people on the map at the same moment" can mean

Interpretation Estimated load Infrastructure impact
A. 15,000 people have the map open, each moving it every 20–30 seconds About 500–750 map searches per second, plus several thousand map-tile requests per second from cache Achievable with caching, several app servers and self-hosted tiles
B. 15,000 map searches in the same second 15,000 requests per second, 50× your 300 RPS target A much larger and more expensive system; not consistent with 9M visits a month
C. 15,000 people logged in anywhere on the site Mostly idle; the load is covered by the 300 RPS target Achievable

We propose to design and test for interpretation A, which is the realistic reading of a busy national portal. Note that A already exceeds 300 requests per second for map searches alone. So we propose the load test uses 300 RPS for general traffic plus the map scenario in A. Please confirm.

Photo storage

4,000,000 photos (200,000 listings × 20). Raw totals before backups:

Average stored size per photo Total
500 KB about 2 TB
1 MB about 4 TB
2 MB about 8 TB
3 MB about 12 TB

PROPOSED: we do not keep full camera originals. On upload, each photo is resized to at most 2,048 pixels wide and converted to WebP, and we generate three versions: thumbnail (about 20 KB), medium (about 120 KB) and large (about 400 KB). That is about 550 KB per photo, about 2.2 TB in total. A daily backup copy roughly doubles the storage. If you want originals kept, add the 8–12 TB rows above.

Monthly outbound traffic

If an average visit loads about 2 MB of images and map data, 9M visits produce about 18–25 TB of outbound traffic per month. On Infomaniak, instance bandwidth is free, but object-storage outbound traffic above 10 TB per month is billed (Infomaniak pricing). We therefore propose a caching layer in front of photo storage (section 11) to keep that cost low.

5. Proposed architecture

The platform is split into independent parts, so each one can grow on its own: the website, the application servers, the database, photo storage, and the map. The application servers keep no user data in memory, so we can add more of them without changing code.

System diagram

In plain words: visitors reach a load balancer, which sends each request to the right place. Page requests go to the Node.js servers, photos come from a cache in front of object storage, and map images come from our own tile server. Heavy work (imports, photo resizing, the 30-day expiry) runs in background workers so the website stays fast.

Deployment on Infomaniak

We propose Infomaniak Public Cloud. It is built on OpenStack (itself open source), runs in Swiss data centres across 2 regions and 6 availability zones, and provides managed load balancers, S3-compatible object storage, DDoS protection and autoscaling (Infomaniak Public Cloud).

  • Three environments: development, staging (a copy of production for testing), and production.
  • Infrastructure as code: the whole setup is written as scripts (OpenTofu/Terraform and Ansible, both supported by Infomaniak). This is what makes a 2-hour rebuild possible.

6. Database architecture

PostgreSQL holds all text data and coordinates; PostGIS adds fast location search. Photos are stored in object storage, and the database keeps only their addresses (URLs). At 200,000 listings the database is about 1–2 GB and fits in server memory, which keeps queries fast.

Entity relationship diagram (initial)

Main tables

Table Holds Key fields
users Private users, company staff, admins email, password hash, role, verified_at
companies Real-estate companies name, logo, description, verified, show_in_subheader
company_users Which users act for which company company_id, user_id, company role
listings Properties title, description, price, rooms, size, address, location (PostGIS point), status, published_at, expires_at, archived_at
property_types Apartment, house, room, office, land, etc. name, parent
listing_images Photo references listing_id, storage key, sort order, sizes
amenities / listing_amenities Balcony, parking, lift, etc. many-to-many
favorites Saved listings per user user_id, listing_id
saved_searches Stored filters + map area user_id, filters, notify flag
contact_requests Messages to the advertiser listing_id, sender, message
listing_reports Abuse or fraud flags listing_id, reason, status
imports / import_rows Each CSV/Excel upload and its row results file, status, counts; row data, errors
audit_logs Who changed what, when actor, action, target, timestamp

Why a PostGIS point with a spatial index

Each listing stores its location as a PostGIS point (longitude, latitude). A spatial index (GiST) lets the database answer "which listings are inside the area on screen?" without scanning all 200,000 rows. This is the single most important performance decision for the map. FACT: it is the standard PostGIS approach for this kind of query.

Other indexing decisions

  • A partial index covering only active listings, since visitors never search expired ones.
  • Indexes on the common filters: property type, price, rooms, status and expiry date.
  • Full-text search using PostgreSQL's built-in text search. No separate search engine (such as Elasticsearch) is needed at launch. PROPOSED: we revisit this only if search needs grow.
  • A connection pool (PgBouncer) between the application and the database, so traffic peaks do not exhaust database connections.

7. Users, flows, and the 30-day listing cycle

The platform has six roles. Each listing moves through a fixed set of states, and one scheduled job moves it from live to expired after 30 days.

User roles

Role Can do
Visitor (not logged in) Search, filter, use the map, view listings and company pages, contact advertisers (if allowed without account)
Registered user All of the above, plus publish and manage own listings, favourites, saved searches
Company staff Publish and manage listings for their company

Moderator Review listings and reports, hide content

Main user flow: publishing a listing

The visitor's flow is simpler: search by place or move the map, filter, open a listing, contact the advertiser or save it.

Listing lifecycle

NEEDS YOUR DECISION: Should every listing be reviewed before going live, only listings from private users, or none? Should companies be trusted to publish directly?

How the 30-day expiry job works

Timestamps on every listing: created_at, updated_at, published_at, expires_at, expired_at, archived_at, deleted_at. All times are stored in UTC.

The job (runs every hour):

  1. Takes a lock so only one server runs it, even when we have several.
  2. Finds published listings where expires_at has passed, in batches of 1,000.
  3. Sets them to Expired. They leave search and the map at once.
  4. Queues the owner notification email.
  5. Moves listings expired longer than the grace period to Archived.
  6. Permanently deletes listings archived longer than the retention period, then deletes their photos from storage.
  7. Records what it did. If the job fails to run, monitoring sends an alert.

The job is safe to repeat: running it twice changes nothing the second time. Renewal simply sets expires_at to 30 days from now.

PROPOSED notifications: 7 days before expiry, 1 day before, and on expiry, each with a one-click renew link.

Why archive before deleting: instant deletion is irreversible. A bug, a mistaken date or a bad import could erase thousands of live listings. Archiving first lets us restore, handle disputes and let owners renew.

NEEDS YOUR DECISION: grace period and retention period. Our suggestion is 30 days in Expired, then 90 days in Archived, then permanent deletion. Local law in your target country may require keeping some records longer.

9. Map architecture

We build the map entirely from OpenStreetMap data processed and served on your own Infomaniak servers. No Google, Mapbox or other commercial map account is needed, and no licence fee is owed. The cost is one or two servers and the work to keep them updated.

Four separate things people call "OpenStreetMap"

Item What it is Our use
OSM data The world's streets, buildings and places, free under the ODbL licence We download your country's data file
OSM public tile servers Volunteer servers that draw map images Not used — forbidden for heavy use, no SLA
OSM public geocoder Volunteer address-search service (Nominatim) Not used — limited to 1 request per second
Self-hosted OSM tiles and geocoder The same software and data on our servers Used

How the map is built and served

The map background (streets, parks, place names) is prepared in advance as a single file and served as static data, which is very cheap to serve at high traffic. We rebuild it on a schedule (PROPOSED: monthly) so new streets appear. The property pins are a separate layer, fetched live from PostGIS.

Showing 200,000 properties without slowing down

The browser never receives all 200,000 pins. For the visible area and zoom level, the server returns clusters (a bubble saying "124 properties") when zoomed out, and individual pins when zoomed in. Results for popular areas are cached for a short time. This keeps every map request small and fast.

Map display library — NEEDS YOUR DECISION

You specified Leaflet. It works, and we can use it. For your traffic level we want you to know the trade-off before you decide.

Option How it works Pros Cons
A. Leaflet + image tiles Server draws map images Simple, familiar, very mature Server must render and store many images: more CPU and storage
B. MapLibre GL JS + vector tiles (our recommendation) Server sends compact map data; the visitor's device draws it Less server load, smoother zoom, handles many pins well Slightly more complex styling
C. Leaflet + MapLibre bridge plugin Leaflet controls with vector tiles underneath Keeps Leaflet An extra plugin to maintain

All three options are open source with no fees. We recommend B because it moves most of the drawing work to visitors' devices, which directly lowers your server cost at 15,000 concurrent map users.

Address search (geocoding)

  • Imports and new listings need addresses converted to coordinates. We run Nominatim ourselves, loaded with your country's data only.
  • For one country the server needs are moderate. For comparison, a full-planet Nominatim needs 128 GB RAM or more and at least 1 TB of fast disk (Nominatim installation guide). We do not need the whole planet unless you plan to go global.
  • For the search box "type a town and see suggestions", Nominatim is not designed for autocomplete. PROPOSED: a simple table of towns and postcodes in our own database, or the open-source Photon geocoder. We confirm the choice in the design phase.

Attribution you must display

The map corner must show "© OpenStreetMap contributors". If we use the OpenMapTiles map layout (the most common one), it must also show "© OpenMapTiles" (OpenMapTiles licence). This is a legal credit requirement, not a fee.

NEEDS YOUR DECISION: Which country or countries will the platform cover at launch? This determines the map data size, server size and address formats.

10. CSV / Excel import architecture

Imports run as a controlled pipeline: nothing reaches the live site until the file has been checked, previewed and confirmed. Every imported listing remembers which import created it, so a bad import can be undone in one step.

Import pipeline

What each step does

Step Purpose
File check Rejects wrong formats, oversized files and broken encodings before any processing
Column mapping Matches the file's columns ("Prix", "Price", "Rent/month") to our fields. A company's mapping is remembered for its next import
Normalise Converts formats: "1'200.-", "1200 CHF" and "1,200" all become 1200
Geocode Converts each address to coordinates. Rows that cannot be located are flagged, not silently placed at a wrong spot
Duplicate check Uses the company's own reference number when present; otherwise address + price + size similarity
Preview Shows counts of valid rows, warnings and errors; errors downloadable as a spreadsheet to fix and re-upload
Write Inserts or updates in batches of about 1,000 rows, inside transactions
Undo Removes all listings created by a given import

We provide a standard template file, but the mapping step means companies can also upload their own formats.

Re-imports and updates

If a company uploads its inventory again, rows with a known reference number update the existing listing (and renew its 30 days, if you choose). New references create new listings. NEEDS YOUR DECISION: should listings missing from a new file be expired automatically?

Photos in imports — NEEDS YOUR DECISION

A spreadsheet cannot contain photos. Two common options:

  1. The file contains photo web links; our workers download them. Simple for agencies whose photos are already online.
  2. The company uploads a ZIP file of photos named by reference number.

The initial load of 200,000 listings

The first large import is an operation to plan, not a button click. Geocoding 200,000 addresses on our own geocoder takes roughly a few hours. Processing about 4 million photos (download, resize into three sizes, store) takes roughly 1–3 days of background processing depending on server capacity. We schedule it before launch, with progress tracking. These are estimates to confirm with a trial import of real data.

When APIs become necessary (future)

Only if agencies want automatic, continuous synchronisation from their own software, or if another portal must exchange data with yours. The import pipeline above is designed so a future API feed can reuse the same validation steps.

11. Photo and storage architecture

Photos live in Infomaniak's S3-compatible object storage; the database stores only a short reference to each file. Photos are resized once at upload and then served from a cache, so the application servers never handle photo traffic.

Why photos must not go into PostgreSQL

If photos are stored in the database If photos are stored in object storage
Database grows from about 2 GB to several TB Database stays about 2 GB and fits in memory
Every backup and restore copies terabytes, so a 2-hour recovery becomes impossible Database restores in minutes; photos are backed up separately
Every photo view goes through the application and database Photos are served directly from a cache
Storage costs more (database-grade disks) Object storage is the cheapest storage tier

Upload and processing

  1. The user uploads a photo; it is stored temporarily and a background job is queued.
  2. The worker checks it is a real image by reading the file content, not trusting the file name. Size limits apply.
  3. Location metadata (GPS data in the photo file) is removed to protect the owner's privacy.
  4. Three versions are created (thumbnail, medium, large) in WebP format using the open-source sharp library.
  5. Files are saved to object storage under a unique name that never changes, and the reference is saved in the database.

Serving photos

An Nginx cache sits in front of object storage. Because file names never change, browsers and the cache can keep photos for a long time. Most photo views never reach the storage at all. This keeps the site fast and keeps object-storage outbound traffic below Infomaniak's 10 TB monthly free allowance as much as possible (section 4).

PROPOSED (Phase 2): a second cache node for redundancy. A content delivery network (CDN) is optional; if used, choose a European provider so photos are not routed through US services.

NEEDS YOUR DECISION: maximum photos per listing (you said an average of 20; we suggest a hard limit of 30) and whether original photos must be kept.

14. Backup and disaster recovery

Your 2-hour recovery target is achievable, but only with scripted infrastructure, backups stored in a second location, and recovery drills we actually time. Daily backups alone do not deliver it, and on their own they can lose up to 24 hours of data.

Two numbers that define recovery

Term Meaning Your target Our proposal
RTO (Recovery Time Objective) How long until the site works again Live map within 2 hours Under 2 hours for listings, search and map
RPO (Recovery Point Objective) How much recent data can be lost Not stated — NEEDS YOUR DECISION Under 15 minutes

With only a nightly backup, a failure at 23:00 loses everything since the previous night: new listings, sign-ups, edits. That is why we add continuous backup of the database log (below).

What is backed up, and how

Data Method Frequency Stored where
Database Full backup with pgBackRest (open source) Daily Second Infomaniak region
Database changes Continuous transaction-log archiving Every few minutes Second region
Photos Copy of new and changed files (photos never change, so only new ones are copied) Daily, plus the initial full copy Second region
Map tile file and geocoder data Copy of the built files (rebuilding them takes hours) After each monthly rebuild Second region
Configuration and infrastructure scripts Version-controlled code repository Every change Repository + backup

PROPOSED retention: 7 daily, 4 weekly and 12 monthly database backups. Backups are encrypted and cannot be deleted with the production servers' credentials, so a hacked server cannot erase them.

NEEDS YOUR DECISION: Do you also want a copy outside Infomaniak, for protection against a provider-wide problem? It adds cost and a second provider.

Recovery after a total failure — target timeline

Time Action
0–15 min Alert received, failure confirmed, recovery started
15–45 min Servers recreated in the other region from our infrastructure scripts
30–60 min Database restored from backup and replayed to the latest point (about 2 GB, so minutes)
30–60 min Map tile file and geocoder restored; application deployed
60–90 min Traffic switched to the new servers; checks on search, map and login
90–120 min Buffer for problems

Photos are the exception. Copying about 2 TB takes too long for a 2-hour window, so during a recovery the photo cache reads directly from the backup copy. Listings, search and the map come back first; photos follow as the cache fills.

Failure scenarios

Scenario Recovery method Expected recovery time
One server fails Load balancer or standby takes over Seconds to minutes (Phase 2)
One availability zone fails Standby database in another zone Minutes (Phase 2)
Data deleted by mistake Restore to the moment before the mistake Under 1 hour
Whole region fails Rebuild in second region (timeline above) Under 2 hours
Hacked or encrypted by ransomware Rebuild on clean servers from protected backups Under 2 hours after the breach is contained

Proof, not promises

  • Weekly: an automatic test restores the latest database backup to a test server and checks it.
  • Before launch, then every quarter: a full recovery drill, timed, with a written report to you.
  • A written recovery runbook, so any qualified engineer can follow it.
